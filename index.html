      How this is calculated

      Every input comes from the data on the other tabs; nothing is entered by hand.

      1. Each race starts from an expected margin. Cook’s rating becomes a margin: Toss Up 0 points, Lean 7, Likely 10, Solid 17. Where RealClearPolitics publishes a Senate polling average, the expected margin is halfway between that average and the rating’s margin. House seats use the rating alone, since the tracker has no district polls.
      2. Races can miss together and separately. Each race gets its own error (standard deviation 4.5 points) plus one nationwide error shared by every race (3 points), because polls and ratings tend to miss in the same direction in a given year. Combined that is about ±5.4 points, so a 2-point lead is still close to a coin flip.
      3. The error sizes match the raters’ record. With them, the favorite wins about 90% of Lean races, 97% of Likely and 99.9% of Solid, and Toss Ups split evenly. A study of Cook’s August House ratings from 1984 to 2008 found 85.7%, 94.9% and 99.8%, with toss-ups splitting about evenly (Campbell, 2010). Ratings this close to Election Day have a better record (Cook), so the tracker sets them a little higher.
      4. Winning both. Democrats need — Senate seats (the — they hold that aren’t on the ballot plus the races they win; a win by independent Dan Osborn in Nebraska isn’t counted) and — House seats. On their own the model gives Democrats — in the Senate and — in the House. The two are worked out together rather than multiplied, because the same nationwide miss moves every race in both chambers: a year good enough for a Senate majority is usually good enough for the House too.
      5. Worked out exactly. The chance is summed over every size of nationwide miss instead of by random simulation, so it doesn’t change when you reload the page.

      A chance is not a call: at 70%, the other side still wins about 3 times in 10.

      About this tracker

      Race ratings come from the Cook Political Report unless a card says otherwise. Polling averages are RealClearPolitics averages as reported by Election Central, and every individual poll names its pollster. News items are summarized rather than quoted, and each names its source. The Odds tab is the tracker’s own estimate, calculated from those ratings and averages; that tab explains how.

      Compiled with help from Claude, an AI model, from public sources. It can contain errors, so check the original source before relying on a figure. Not affiliated with any campaign, party or rating organization.
